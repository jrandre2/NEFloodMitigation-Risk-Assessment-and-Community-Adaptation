## Who Owns the Flood Plain: A Parcel-Level Analysis of Organizational Form and Flood Risk

## Abstract
Regulatory flood maps define the geography of hazard but obscure the organizational structure of exposure. This study examines how regulatory flood exposure is distributed across owner-of-record forms and local portfolio footprints using the complete 2022 inventory of single-family residential parcels in Douglas County, Nebraska (N=183,313). We classify owners into five organizational forms (including Individuals, LLCs, and Corporations) and distinguish between single-parcel and multi-parcel local portfolios. While only 1.10% of parcels fall within FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs) in aggregate, prevalence varies sharply by ownership structure. Unadjusted SFHA rates are highest among single-parcel Limited Liability Companies (4.83%) and single-parcel corporations (4.07%), compared to 0.81% for single-parcel individuals. In modified Poisson models with neighborhood fixed effects, LLC-owned parcels retain elevated exposure relative to individuals (adjusted Risk Ratio for single-parcel LLCs = 1.67; 95% CI: 1.31–2.13). Multi-parcel individuals and trusts also exhibit elevated prevalence. These findings demonstrate that flood exposure is not just a spatial attribute but an organizational one, concentrated within specific legal structures. Consequently, effective flood governance requires implementation strategies capable of navigating diverse legal forms and portfolio scales for mitigation and compliance.

**Keywords:** Flood Exposure; Ownership Structure; Urban Analytics; Limited Liability Companies (LLCs); Floodplain Management; Local Portfolio Footprint

## 1. Introduction

Flooding is among the most common and costly hazards affecting U.S. communities with local governments increasingly rely on regulatory flood maps to guide land-use decisions, building requirements, and risk communication (Burby 2001; Lim & Skidmore 2019). Yet flood maps primarily answer a geographic question, where regulatory hazard is located, while leaving a parallel governance question under-specified: who holds that mapped exposure. This ownership blind spot matters because the implementation of floodplain governance is mediated through owners. Many of the key levers available to cities and counties, including enforcement of floodplain development standards, targeted outreach and mitigation assistance, participation in buyouts or property elevations, and post-disaster repair and recovery coordination, ultimately depend on identifying, contacting, and negotiating with the entities that own the exposed housing stock. In other words, hazard is spatial, but governance is organizational: regulatory exposure is administered through property owners and the legal forms through which they hold assets.

A growing literature shows that flood risk is not always fully reflected in housing market signals. Evidence of imperfect risk capitalization and informational frictions implies that the distribution of flood exposure across owners may not mirror a frictionless, actuarially priced market. But the significance of ownership is not limited to any single pricing mechanism. Even if flood risk were perfectly understood and priced, owners would still differ in their capacity and willingness to manage, insure, mitigate, or exit flood-exposed property. These differences are institutional: legal organizational form shapes liability, financing and underwriting pathways, administrative legibility, and compliance strategies, while portfolio footprint shapes operational capacity, diversification, and bargaining power with contractors, insurers, and regulators. From a governance perspective, knowing that an exposed parcel is owned by a single household versus a business entity, a trust, or a nonprofit is not a mere descriptive curiosity; it is information about the implementation interface through which regulation and policy must operate.

Despite its importance, ownership is often represented using coarse proxies that blur analytically meaningful distinctions. Common operationalizations, such as absentee-owner flags derived from mailing address mismatches or neighborhood-level indicators such as renter shares, collapse very different actors into the same category and can conflate geographic distance with institutional form. These simplifications obscure differences between, for example, an out-of-town individual owner, a local trust holding property for estate planning, a multi-parcel corporation operating at scale, and an LLC created to hold a single asset. They also complicate comparisons across studies because thresholds and definitions for “investors,” “institutions,” and “corporate ownership” vary widely across the housing and urban policy literatures. What is needed is a tractable, transferable way to make ownership structure legible at parcel scale, using information that is broadly available in local administrative records, and to evaluate whether regulatory flood exposure is systematically organized across these ownership structures.

This study addresses that measurement gap by linking parcel-level regulatory flood exposure to a decision-relevant ownership typology in a single metropolitan county. Using the complete set of single-family residential parcels in Douglas County, Nebraska, we classify each parcel’s owner-of-record into five mutually exclusive organizational forms, including Individuals, Limited Liability Companies (LLCs), Corporations, Trusts, and Government/Nonprofit entities, and measure each owner’s local portfolio footprint based on the number of parcels held under the same legal name within the county. We then overlay parcels with FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs) to construct a binary indicator of regulatory flood exposure and estimate conditional differences in SFHA prevalence across ownership structures, adjusting for parcel characteristics and neighborhood context. In the 2022 assessment roll used here, the overall prevalence of SFHA location among single-family parcels is low, about 1.1%, but the central question is whether that exposure, though rare in aggregate, is disproportionately concentrated within particular organizational forms and portfolio footprints, and whether that pattern persists after accounting for confounding differences in parcel value, parcel size, and micro-location.

Our framing is governance-forward and observational. We do not argue that organizational form causes parcels to be in floodplains; floodplains are determined by hydrology and regulatory mapping, not by ownership labels. Instead, we treat ownership as a correlate of selection into, retention of, and organization of regulatory exposure. This distinction is important for interpretation. Differences in SFHA prevalence across owner types can arise through multiple pathways, such as acquisition strategies, financing constraints, risk tolerance, operational specialization, or the use of legal entities to manage liability and administrative exposure, many of which cannot be directly observed in a cross-sectional assessor snapshot. Accordingly, we use cautious language throughout and interpret results as conditional associations that make the distribution of regulatory exposure across owners empirically visible and comparable.

Within this governance-oriented frame, legal form and local portfolio footprint provide two observable dimensions that plausibly organize exposure in different ways. Business entities, specifically LLCs and corporations, may hold relatively more SFHA-designated property for reasons that do not require any strong assumption about “profitability” of risky parcels: they may face different financing channels and insurance constraints, have greater capacity to manage compliance and repair, diversify risk across a larger set of holdings, or pursue acquisition strategies that differ systematically from individual households. Meanwhile, within-form contrasts by local footprint can be especially revealing because they distinguish small, single-asset entities from owners operating multiple properties. Single parcel LLCs are analytically salient because they can function as asset-level legal shells, potentially compartmentalizing liability or administrative exposure in ways that are distinct from both multi-parcel entities and from individuals, such as liability compartmentalization. At the same time, we acknowledge an important limitation: owner-of-record entities are not the same as ultimate beneficial owners. LLCs may be owned by other entities or individuals, and consolidating beneficial ownership may be desirable for some research and enforcement purposes. Here we retain the owner-of-record unit because it is the entity directly appearing in administrative land records and because the legal shell itself can be meaningful for governance, enforcement, and liability structures, even when beneficial ownership remains obscured.

The paper contributes in three ways. Substantively, it documents whether regulatory flood exposure among single-family parcels is disproportionately held by particular owner forms and portfolio footprints, and whether SFHA holdings are diffuse or concentrated within each ownership form. Conceptually, it advances a decision and implementation-oriented view of ownership as a missing layer in urban hazard analytics: flood governance requires not only mapping where exposure is located but also describing how that exposure is organized across owners who differ in legal structure and operating scale. Methodologically, it demonstrates a replicable workflow for transforming owner-of-record name strings and assessor records into a structured ownership typology that can be joined to regulatory hazard layers, while keeping the focus on the governance-relevant empirical patterns rather than on classification novelty.

We organize the analysis around three linked questions: first, how do unadjusted SFHA rates differ across organizational forms and between owners with single parcel versus multi-parcel local footprints; second, do these differences persist when conditioning on parcel characteristics and neighborhood context; and third, within organizational forms, do single-parcel and multi-parcel entities exhibit distinct patterns of regulatory exposure that are consistent with different organizational strategies?

These questions motivate two hypotheses and one within-form research question:

H1: Organizational Form. Conditional on parcel characteristics and neighborhood context, parcels owned by business entities, including LLCs and corporations, exhibit higher prevalence of SFHA location than parcels owned by individuals. This hypothesis reflects the expectation that institutional owners differ from households in constraints and capacities relevant to holding and managing regulated hazard exposure.

H2: Within-LLC Footprint. Among LLC-owned parcels, parcels held by single-parcel LLCs exhibit higher prevalence of SFHA location than parcels held by multi-parcel LLCs. This hypothesis treats single-parcel LLCs as a distinctive organizational pattern that may be consistent with asset-level structuring, such as liability compartmentalization, and/or other single-asset strategies.

RQ1: Within-form Heterogeneity Beyond LLCs. Do within-form differences by local portfolio footprint vary across corporations, trusts, and government/nonprofit owners, and if so, in what direction? We approach this as an exploratory within-form comparison because portfolio footprint may relate to exposure differently depending on the legal and operational characteristics of each organizational form.

In addition to these form and footprint contrasts, we provide a complementary descriptive assessment of concentration, examining how SFHA-designated parcels are distributed across owners within each organizational form, because the administrative tractability of governance interventions depends not only on average prevalence differences but also on whether exposure is held by many small owners or by a relatively small number of larger portfolios.

## 2. Literature Review 

# 2.1 Regulatory flood Exposure 

Regulatory flood maps translate hydrologic risk into administrable categories that guide planning, permitting, and program implementation. However, maps and parcel overlays primarily describe where regulatory exposure lies; they do not, by themselves, describe how that exposure is organized across owners. This distinction is consequential because most governance interventions are implemented through property owners rather than through parcels in the abstract. Ownership structure shapes the feasibility of targeted outreach, compliance support, and enforcement, and it affects whether exposures are administratively diffuse (many small owners) or concentrated (a smaller number of large portfolios). Work on landlord scale and targeted code enforcement has emphasized that interventions often depend on the ability to identify and engage owners at meaningful organizational units rather than treating each parcel as independent (An et al., 2024). More broadly, research on rental property owner decision-making underscores that owners are heterogeneous in motivations, constraints, and operational capacity, and that typologies can improve the linkage between observable owner characteristics and governance-relevant outcomes (Cook et al., 2024).

At the same time, a large and growing literature suggests that flood risk is not always fully reflected in housing market signals, implying that selection into flood-exposed assets may not align with actuarially fair pricing. Hedonic studies show that flood hazards and flood experiences can be associated with price penalties in affected areas, indicating some degree of risk capitalization (Bin & Polasky, 2004; Atreya & Ferreira, 2015). Yet national evidence also indicates that climate and flood risks can remain insufficiently priced, producing overvaluation in aggregate and implying persistent information gaps and/or constraints among market participants (Gourevitch et al., 2023). For the present study, the key implication is not that any single pricing mechanism must dominate, but that imperfect signals can contribute to heterogeneous sorting and retention—making it empirically and practically important to understand which owners hold regulatory flood exposure.

A further complication is that ownership itself can be opaque. Properties are often held through layered entities, nominee structures, and shell companies that impede straightforward identification of the organizational units relevant for administration and accountability. Recent housing scholarship has documented the problem of “ownership obscurity” and developed strategies to link records across legal entities and networks (Hangen & O’Brien, 2024; Shelton & Seymour, 2024; Torres, 2024). These measurement challenges reinforce the governance motivation for tractable, replicable approaches that can translate owner-of-record strings into structured ownership information at scale.

# 2.2 Ownership structure and the organization of regulatory exposure

# 2.2.1 Institutional strategies and holding of regulated hazard

Institutional participation in single-family housing expanded markedly after the foreclosure crisis, with professionalized investors acquiring homes in distressed submarkets and scaling portfolio operations (Immergluck & Law, 2014a, 2014b; Colburn et al., 2021). Empirical work on single-family rentals shows that returns vary across markets and segments, which can support geographically differentiated acquisition strategies and portfolio allocation (Demers & Eisfeldt, 2022). City- and market-specific profiles of large investors similarly show that corporate ownership can have a distinct spatial footprint that is salient for local governance (Ding et al., 2025). Together, this literature suggests that business entities—especially those operating at larger portfolio scales—may not be randomly distributed across the housing stock, and that their holdings can cluster in particular neighborhoods and market segments.

The floodplain is one such segment where institutional selection could plausibly occur. Flood hazard designation and flood experiences have been associated with price effects, implying that flood-exposed parcels may be relatively less expensive than otherwise similar properties in some contexts (Bin & Polasky, 2004; Atreya & Ferreira, 2015). At the same time, national evidence that climate risk is insufficiently capitalized implies that discounts may be incomplete relative to expected losses, creating conditions under which a subset of actors may be willing or able to hold regulated exposure even when risks are not fully priced (Gourevitch et al., 2023). Importantly, this does not require a strong assumption that flood-exposed homes are “good deals” on average; rather, it suggests that different owners may face different constraints (e.g., access to capital, ability to diversify across holdings) and may adopt different strategies (e.g., acquisition, renovation, and disposition timing) that make them more likely to select into or retain flood-designated parcels. The analytic implication for this study is that contrasts across organizational forms and within-form portfolio footprints may reveal systematic differences in how regulatory exposure is distributed across owners.

# 2.2.2 Proximity and information as secondary correlates of ownership patterns

A parallel strand of research emphasizes that geographic distance can shape information and management, particularly where ownership is absentee. Work in land management and conservation documents recurring challenges associated with absentee landownership, including weaker local knowledge and engagement (Petrzelka et al., 2013; Ulrich-Schad et al., 2016). In housing, however, distance-based proxies (e.g., “absentee owner” flags) often collapse distinct actor types into a single category and risk conflating geography with institutional form. At the same time, digital technologies and platform-mediated management can reduce some costs of distance and enable remote ownership at scale (Fields, 2022). Because proximity can correlate with both owner type and management strategy, it is best treated as a secondary correlate that may moderate or mediate observed ownership–exposure associations rather than as a complete substitute for organizational measures. Consistent with that view, recent work in Douglas County documents a strong gradient between owner proximity and parcel-level flood exposure, motivating proximity as a robustness consideration rather than the primary construct of interest (Andrews et al., 2025).

# 2.2.3 Legal form, liability, and administrative legibility

Beyond acquisition and proximity, legal structure can shape how risk is managed and how governance is implemented. A synthesis of the rental housing literature highlights that owners differ in decision-making processes and constraints, and that organizational attributes can be linked to observable outcomes even when underlying motivations are not directly measured (Cook et al., 2024). Empirically, research on rental properties and maintenance indicates that property condition and code compliance can vary across owner types and tenures (Hwang 2019; Rose & Harris, 2022), and recent work connects single-family rental investor type to property conditions and neighborhood implications (Harrison et al., 2025). These studies do not imply that any particular legal form maps cleanly onto rental status, but they do suggest that organizational form and operating scale can correlate with management practices relevant for hazard exposure and recovery.

Legal form is also relevant to liability and administrative legibility. LLCs, in particular, have been linked to patterns of disinvestment and “organized neglect,” and the use of limited-liability entities can complicate accountability and enforcement by fragmenting responsibility across nominal owners (Travis, 2019). This literature motivates attention to within-form heterogeneity, especially the distinction between single-asset entities and entities that hold multiple properties, because the former can function as parcel-level legal shells even when beneficial control is more consolidated (Travis, 2019). For flood governance, these distinctions matter because they shape the unit through which regulators, program administrators, and community partners must engage to implement mitigation, compliance, and post-event recovery coordination.

# 2.3 Measuring ownership structure in administrative records

A practical barrier to ownership-centered flood governance research is measurement: local administrative data typically include owner-of-record name strings rather than standardized organizational attributes. Recent work shows that legal form can be inferred from name morphology using modern language models. For example, transformer-based classifiers trained on large, labeled corpora have demonstrated strong performance in predicting entity legal form from names, outperforming traditional baselines (Arimond et al., 2023). Related benchmarking work finds that hybrid approaches combining machine learning with rules-based extraction can improve legal-form classification and entity matching relative to rules-only systems (Kruse et al., 2021). In parallel, urban analytics research has demonstrated the feasibility of applying deep learning methods to large-scale urban text corpora while documenting the benefits and tradeoffs of such approaches (Lore et al., 2024).

NLP-based processing of administrative records is now common in housing research, supporting the broader feasibility of extracting structured information from unstructured government text at scale. For instance, Thomas et al. (2024) apply OCR and named-entity recognition workflows to eviction filings to support systematic data collection and neighborhood analysis. Together, this measurement literature supports the premise that owner-of-record name strings can be transformed into structured legal-form categories in a replicable way, enabling comparative analyses that go beyond coarse proxies such as absentee flags.

# 2.4 Ownership typologies: legal shells, beneficial owners, and local portfolio footprint

Most empirical housing work that studies “investor” or “institutional” ownership relies on simplified typologies (e.g., corporate vs. non-corporate owners) and then bins owners by portfolio size. However, definitions and thresholds vary substantially, complicating cross-study comparisons and governance translation (Goodman et al., 2023; U.S. GAO, 2024). In response, an emerging measurement literature has emphasized methods to identify ownership scale and reduce obscurity by linking entities across shell structures and networks. “Who Owns America?” offers a methodology for identifying landlords’ ownership scale with direct implications for targeted code enforcement (An et al., 2024). Complementary approaches develop linkage strategies to uncover obscured landlord identities (Hangen & O’Brien, 2024), map networks of corporate landlords (Shelton & Seymour, 2024), and propose methods to group and unmask anonymous corporate owners in administrative data (Torres, 2024).

This study is designed to complement—rather than compete with—beneficial-owner consolidation approaches. We retain the owner-of-record legal shell as a meaningful administrative unit because it is the entity appearing in land records and can be consequential for governance and liability. The empirical importance of shell entities has been demonstrated in work showing how LLC structures can organize disinvestment and complicate accountability (Travis, 2019). At the same time, we incorporate a second dimension—local portfolio footprint—because the administrative tractability and potential policy leverage of outreach or enforcement often depend on whether exposure is held by many small owners or concentrated among fewer, larger portfolios (An et al., 2024). Together, legal form and local footprint constitute a decision-relevant typology that is observable in assessor data, scalable to large administrative files, and interpretable for governance contexts, while acknowledging that beneficial ownership may be more consolidated than the owner-of-record layer suggests (Hangen & O’Brien, 2024; Torres, 2024).

# 2.5 Synthesis and hypotheses

The literature above motivates a governance-forward empirical question: whether regulatory flood exposure is systematically organized across ownership structures that differ in legal form and portfolio footprint. Research on post-crisis investor activity and institutional single-family ownership suggests that business entities can exhibit distinctive acquisition and portfolio strategies that are spatially patterned and operationally consequential (Immergluck & Law, 2014a, 2014b; Colburn et al., 2021; Demers & Eisfeldt, 2022; Ding et al., 2025). Evidence on flood risk capitalization indicates that flood hazard is reflected in prices in some settings but may remain insufficiently priced in aggregate, creating conditions under which heterogeneous sorting and retention can occur (Bin & Polasky, 2004; Atreya & Ferreira, 2015; Gourevitch et al., 2023). Work on legal form and accountability highlights that LLC structures can fragment responsibility and organize risk-relevant outcomes through nominal ownership (Travis, 2019). Finally, research on absentee ownership and remote management suggests that proximity can correlate with information and management capacity, though modern technologies may partially offset distance costs (Petrzelka et al., 2013; Ulrich-Schad et al., 2016; Fields, 2022).

Guided by these strands, we test two pre-specified hypotheses and one within-form research question using parcel-level data:

H1 (Organizational form): Conditional on parcel characteristics and neighborhood context, parcels owned by business entities (LLCs and corporations) exhibit higher prevalence of SFHA location than parcels owned by individuals. This hypothesis is motivated by evidence that institutional and professionalized owners operate with distinct portfolio strategies and capacities relative to households (Immergluck & Law, 2014a, 2014b; Colburn et al., 2021; Demers & Eisfeldt, 2022).

H2 (Within-LLC footprint): Among LLC-owned parcels, parcels held by single-parcel LLCs exhibit higher prevalence of SFHA location than parcels held by multi-parcel LLCs. This hypothesis is motivated by evidence that LLC structures can compartmentalize accountability and facilitate fragmentation of responsibility across nominal owners (Travis, 2019).

RQ3 (Within-form heterogeneity beyond LLCs): Do within-form differences by local portfolio footprint vary across corporations, trusts, and government/nonprofit owners, and if so, in what direction? This question recognizes that portfolio footprint may relate differently to exposure depending on the legal and operational characteristics of each form (An et al., 2024; Cook et al., 2024).

In addition to these prevalence contrasts, the literature on ownership scale and obscurity motivates attention to concentration: governance capacity depends not only on average differences across forms, but also on whether SFHA holdings are diffuse or concentrated among a small subset of owners within each category (An et al., 2024; Hangen & O’Brien, 2024; Shelton & Seymour, 2024).

## 3. Data & Methods

# 3.1 Study area, data sources, and analytical sample

This study examines the universe of single-family residential (SFR) parcels in Douglas County, Nebraska, using the county’s 2022 assessment roll linked to parcel geometries. Douglas County is located in eastern Nebraska along the Missouri River and anchors the Omaha metropolitan region. The county encompasses 326 square miles of land and had an estimated 601,158 residents inas of 2024, reflecting a relatively dense, highly urbanized setting for a Great Plains jurisdiction (U.S. Census Bureau, n.d.). Hydrologically, much of the county’s urban drainage is organized around the Papillion Creek watershed, an approximately 402 mi² basin spanning Douglas, Sarpy, and Washington counties; major tributaries include Big Papillion, Little Papillion, West Papillion, and Papillion Creeks, and watershed flows ultimately discharge to the Missouri River south of Bellevue (Papillion Creek Watershed Partnership, n.d.). This basin is among the most flood-prone and heavily populated areas in Nebraska and has a long history of damaging floods and structural flood-control interventions, including levees and channel modifications along major reaches (Strauch & Hoefer, 2025). Against this geographic and governance backdrop, our analysis draws on the county’s 2022 assessment roll and focuses on the universe of single-family residential parcels (N = 184,333); under our majority-area overlay, mapped SFHA exposure is relatively uncommon (≈1.1% of SFR parcels), but its distribution across ownership forms and local portfolio footprints is central to the study’s motivating question.

The analytical sample is restricted to parcels classified as single-family residential in the assessor data. Parcels are excluded only when required for estimation—for example, when geometries needed for the floodplain overlay are invalid or when key covariates used in regression models are missing. To minimize sample loss attributable to ownership metadata gaps, we apply a metadata fallback procedure that prioritizes the most recent production owner classification and, when necessary, uses auxiliary ownership fields stored with the parcel geometry layer. The preferred fixed-effects specification is estimated on 183,313 SFR parcels; model variants and sample sizes are summarized in Table 5.

# 3.2 Outcome: regulatory flood exposure (SFHA)

The outcome is a binary indicator of regulatory flood exposure, defined as whether a parcel lies within a FEMA SFHA. SFHAs are the mapped regulatory zones that commonly trigger floodplain development standards and flood insurance requirements, making them a policy-relevant boundary for governance even though they do not represent the full set of flood hazards (Kousky, 2018) . Accordingly, the outcome is interpreted as mapped regulatory exposure rather than a direct measure of expected flood losses.

We overlay parcel polygons with FEMA SFHA polygons and classify a parcel as SFHA-exposed if a majority of the parcel area intersects SFHA zones (Zones A/AE/AO/AH). Under this majority-area rule, 2,029 of 184,333 SFR parcels (≈1.10%) are SFHA-exposed in the full county roll. Because some parcels intersect SFHA boundaries only in small slivers, we assess sensitivity to boundary definitions using an alternative exposure rule that classifies a parcel as exposed if at least 10% of its area overlaps an SFHA (described in Section 3.8). Variable definitions and transformations are provided in Table 1.

# 3.3 Ownership measures: organizational form and local portfolio footprint

A central aim of the study is to make ownership structure legible in a form that is relevant for flood governance. We measure ownership along two observable dimensions that are widely available in assessor records: the organizational form of the owner-of-record and the owner’s within-county portfolio footprint. These measures describe the legal owner-of-record—the entity appearing in administrative land records—rather than the consolidated beneficial owner. Legal shells can be governance-relevant in their own right because they shape administrative legibility and liability even when beneficial control is layered through additional entities (Hangen & O’Brien, 2024; Torres, 2024).

Organizational form is classified into five mutually exclusive categories: individuals, limited liability companies (LLCs), corporations, trusts, and government/nonprofit entities. Table 2 summarizes how these categories are identified from owner-of-record strings, the interpretive role they play in this study, and key caveats, including the fact that any category may contain both owner-occupants and landlords. The local portfolio footprint measure captures the number of SFR parcels held under the same owner-of-record name within Douglas County. This jurisdiction-bounded measure reflects the footprint most directly relevant for local administration, while not observing holdings outside the county or holdings distributed across multiple legal shells. For interpretability and statistical power, footprint is binned into single-parcel owners (one SFR parcel) and multi-parcel owners (two or more SFR parcels). Table 3 reports the distribution of these footprint categories by organizational form. Crossing the five organizational forms with the single- versus multi-parcel footprint yields ten ownership groups used in the primary regression models.

# 3.4 Owner-of-record legal form classification

Owner-of-record name fields in assessor data are not standardized, so we classify organizational form using a scalable text-classification workflow. Specifically, we apply a fine-tuned BERT sequence classifier trained on hand-labeled owner names to assign each unique owner-of-record string to one of the five organizational-form categories. Transformer-based approaches are well suited to this task because legal-form signals are embedded in name morphology, abbreviations, and punctuation patterns, and prior benchmarks show that these models can outperform rules-only baselines for legal-form classification (Arimond et al., 2023; Kruse et al., 2021). The classifier produces probabilistic outputs by class; model configuration, training, and coverage are documented in the Supplementary Material (Tables S3–S4 and Figure S1). In addition to using the full production labels for estimation, we re-estimate key models on a high-confidence subset of labels (maximum predicted probability ≥ 0.80) as a robustness check against classification uncertainty (Section 3.8).

# 3.5 Covariates and transformations

To reduce confounding by observable parcel characteristics correlated with both ownership structure and SFHA location, the primary regression models include two parcel controls: total assessed value and parcel size in acres. Both are log-transformed to reduce skew and to better align model fit with the distribution of these variables. Table 1 provides variable definitions, units, and transformations, including an improvement-status indicator used in descriptive splits and sensitivity checks.

As a secondary robustness control, we construct an owner–parcel distance proxy based on the great-circle distance between the parcel centroid and the centroid of the owner’s mailing ZIP code. This term is included as log(1 + distance) in alternative specifications to assess whether ownership–SFHA associations are sensitive to a proxy for owner proximity.

# 3.6 Empirical strategy: parcel-level associations and risk ratios

The primary estimand is the conditional prevalence of SFHA location across ownership structures at the parcel level. The analysis does not treat organizational form as causing parcels to be located in floodplains. Instead, the regression framework estimates how mapped regulatory exposure is distributed across owner-of-record structures after accounting for parcel attributes and micro-location.

We estimate associations using a modified Poisson generalized linear model with a log link for a binary outcome (Zou, 2004). This approach yields coefficients that can be exponentiated and interpreted as risk ratios (RRs), which are often more interpretable than odds ratios when the outcome is rare. Let $Y_{in}$ denote SFHA exposure for parcel $i$ in neighborhood $n$. The preferred specification is:

$$\log(\mathbb{E}[Y_{in}]) = \alpha_n + \sum_g \beta_g \mathbf{1}(G_{in}=g) + \gamma_1 \log(\text{value}_{in}) + \gamma_2 \log(\text{acres}_{in})$$

where $G_{in}$ indexes the ten ownership groups (organizational form × local footprint) and $\alpha_n$ denotes neighborhood fixed effects defined by assessor appraisal neighborhoods. Single-parcel individual owners serve as the reference group. Model variants used for unadjusted comparisons, covariate adjustment, and robustness checks are summarized in Table 5.

Neighborhood fixed effects are included to account for neighborhood-level mean differences that may correlate with both floodplain siting and ownership composition, such as localized infrastructure, development era, and fine-grained topography. Fixed effects are not assumed to remove all spatial dependence in the residuals; they are used to strengthen within-area comparisons at a policy-relevant micro-geographic unit.

A note on estimands: The parcel-level prevalence estimated here is the governance-relevant quantity because floodplain regulation, permitting, and program delivery are administered at the parcel. Multi-parcel owners are mechanically more likely to hold at least one SFHA parcel simply by owning more parcels, but the parcel-level model estimates the share of each group's holdings that fall within SFHAs, a quantity that is comparable across owners regardless of portfolio size. To complement this parcel-level view, the concentration analysis (Section 3.8) provides an owner-level perspective by documenting how SFHA holdings are distributed across owners within each organizational form. As a direct test of whether entity-type associations persist after controlling for portfolio size, the Supplementary Material reports an owner-level Poisson model with an offset for total parcels owned (Table S6); this model confirms that entity-owned portfolios exhibit significantly elevated SFHA intensity relative to individual-owned portfolios even after accounting for differences in portfolio size.

# 3.7 Spatial structure, inference, and diagnostics

Flood exposure and ownership are spatially patterned, so spatial structure is treated as both a substantive feature of the data and an inference concern. We first characterize spatial clustering descriptively using global Moran’s I and local indicators of spatial association (LISA) for SFHA exposure and selected ownership indicators (Moran, 1950; Anselin, 1995), with maps reported in the Supplementary Material (Figures S5–S8).

For regression inference, the preferred models report standard errors clustered at the same appraisal-neighborhood unit used for fixed effects. This approach allows for arbitrary residual correlation within neighborhoods when computing uncertainty intervals and aligns with the governance motivation: neighborhood-level comparisons correspond to the administrative units through which floodplain management is often implemented. Because spatial dependence can persist at scales smaller than neighborhoods, we also assess residual spatial structure by computing Moran's I on primary-model residuals using an 8-nearest-neighbor spatial weights matrix defined on parcel centroids; the resulting diagnostic is reported alongside the main estimates (Table 9). As an additional robustness check, we re-estimate key contrasts using spatial HAC (Conley) standard errors that allow for distance-decay correlation across parcels; results are reported in Table 5 of the supplementary materials (Conley 1999).

To directly address whether a spatial Poisson specification would alter the main findings, we implement eigenvector spatial filtering (ESF) as a robustness analysis (Griffith, 2003). ESF extracts eigenvectors from a row-standardized k-nearest-neighbor spatial weights matrix (k = 8) and includes them as additional controls in the Poisson GLM. These eigenvectors capture spatially structured variation at multiple scales; including progressively more eigenvectors absorbs increasingly fine-grained spatial dependence. We conduct a sensitivity analysis varying the number of spatial filters from 15 to 200 and report the resulting coefficient estimates and residual Moran's I statistics (Table S7). This approach provides a direct test of whether ownership–SFHA associations survive explicit spatial modeling without requiring computationally intensive Bayesian spatial random effects or spatial lag specifications that are poorly supported for count data at this sample size.

# 3.8 Multiple testing, concentration, and robustness checks

The hypothesis tests focus on a small set of prespecified contrasts (Introduction). The primary contrast compares single-parcel LLCs to single-parcel individuals (reference). We also test within-form differences between single- and multi-parcel owners for LLCs, corporations, trusts, and government/nonprofit owners using Wald tests on the relevant coefficient differences. Because multiple within-form tests are conducted, we control the false discovery rate using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995).

To capture the administrative tractability of governance interventions, we quantify how SFHA-exposed parcels are distributed across owners within each organizational form. We construct Lorenz curves and compute Gini coefficients based on owner-of-record holdings, reporting concentration both among owners with at least one SFHA parcel and across all owners in each form (including those with zero SFHA holdings). We also use two-sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests to compare the distributions of SFHA holdings across organizational forms.

Finally, we assess robustness of key estimates to classification uncertainty by re-estimating models on the high-confidence label subset, to floodplain boundary sensitivity by re-estimating using the ≥10% overlap exposure rule, and to owner proximity by adding the distance proxy described above.

## 4. Results

Unless otherwise noted, the unit of analysis is the parcel (not the owner). The outcome is whether a single-family residential (SFR) parcel is located in the regulatory floodplain as defined by FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs). Descriptive rates are computed on the full SFR inventory (N = 184,333), while the preferred neighborhood fixed-effects specification is estimated on a slightly smaller analytic sample due to required covariates and fixed-effect assignment (N = 183,313; Table 5).  

# 4.1 Ownership structure and local portfolio footprint in the SFR stock

SFR parcels in Douglas County are predominantly owned by individuals, but business entities constitute a substantial minority of the housing stock. Individuals hold 82.60% of SFR parcels, followed by LLCs (11.12%), trusts (3.48%), government/nonprofit entities (1.43%), and corporations (1.36%) (Table 4).  

Local portfolio footprint differs sharply by organizational form. Most individually owned parcels are held in single-parcel owner-of-record names (86.32%), whereas LLC and corporate holdings are overwhelmingly multi-parcel within the county (76.96% of LLC parcels; 69.64% of corporate parcels) (Table 3). Trust ownership is closer to individual ownership in this respect (82.54% single-parcel), while government/nonprofit holdings are largely multi-parcel (69.57%) (Table 3). Together, these descriptive distributions underscore that organizational form and within-county footprint capture empirically distinct dimensions of ownership organization in assessor records.  

# 4.2 Unadjusted SFHA prevalence by organizational form and local footprint

Across the full SFR sample, SFHA exposure is rare: 1.10% of parcels fall predominantly within the SFHA (Table 6). However, unadjusted SFHA prevalence differs substantially across the ten organizational forms × footprint groups.  

Two groups have especially high raw SFHA rates: single-parcel LLCs (4.83%; 48.3 per 1,000 parcels) and single-parcel corporations (4.07%; 40.7 per 1,000) (Table 6). Multi-parcel corporations (2.92%; 29.2 per 1,000) and multi-parcel trusts (2.59%; 25.9 per 1,000) also exhibit elevated raw SFHA prevalence relative to the reference group of single-parcel individuals (0.81%; 8.1 per 1,000). Multi-parcel individuals have higher unadjusted exposure than single-parcel individuals (1.25% versus 0.81%), indicating a within-individual footprint gradient in the descriptive data (Table 6). In absolute terms, LLCs account for approximately 23% of all SFHA-located SFR parcels despite holding only 11% of the SFR stock, while single-parcel individuals account for about 53% of SFHA parcels—roughly proportional to their 71% share of total parcels.  

These unadjusted differences motivate the neighborhood fixed-effects models below, which assess whether ownership-group patterns persist within neighborhoods after accounting for parcel size and value.

# 4.3 Adjusted SFHA prevalence in the preferred neighborhood fixed-effects model

Table 7 reports adjusted risk ratios (RRs) from the preferred modified Poisson model with neighborhood fixed effects, controlling for parcel assessed value and parcel area. Uncertainty is computed using standard errors clustered at the neighborhood level, aligning inference with the fixed-effect unit.  

Relative to single-parcel individuals (reference), both LLC groups are over-represented among SFHA parcels in adjusted comparisons. Single-parcel LLC parcels have an RR of 1.670 (95% CI: 1.311–2.126; p < 0.001), and multi-parcel LLC parcels have an RR of 1.379 (95% CI: 1.104–1.722; p = 0.005). In other words, after comparing parcels within the same neighborhood and holding parcel size and assessed value constant, SFHA location is more prevalent among LLC-held parcels than among the baseline category.  

A within-individual footprint gradient also remains after adjustment: multi-parcel individuals have an RR of 1.209 (95% CI: 1.038–1.407; p = 0.015) relative to single-parcel individuals. Multi-parcel trusts are similarly elevated (RR 1.459; 95% CI: 1.043–2.041; p = 0.028), while single-parcel trusts are not statistically distinguishable from the reference group in this specification.  

For corporations, the multi-parcel estimate is positive but imprecise under neighborhood-clustered inference (RR 1.568; 95% CI: 0.960–2.561; p = 0.073). Government/nonprofit parcels show no reliable difference from the reference group in either the single- or multi-parcel categories.  

# 4.4 Within-form footprint contrasts

To evaluate whether portfolio footprint differences within an organizational form are statistically distinguishable, we test equality of the single- versus multi-parcel coefficients within each form (Table 8). Because multiple within-form tests are conducted, Table 8 also reports Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate adjustments.  

Within LLCs, the point estimates indicate higher adjusted SFHA prevalence for single-parcel LLCs than for multi-parcel LLCs, but this within-form contrast is not statistically distinguishable under neighborhood clustering (p = 0.092; BH-FDR p = 0.123). For corporations and trusts, the within-form tests suggest higher SFHA prevalence among multi-parcel portfolios than among single-parcel portfolios, with p-values that are borderline once multiple-testing adjustment is applied (corporations: p = 0.045; BH-FDR p = 0.091; trusts: p = 0.031; BH-FDR p = 0.091). No within-form footprint difference is evident for government/nonprofit owners (p = 0.347).  

# 4.5 Concentration of SFHA holdings across owners

Beyond parcel-level prevalence, SFHA exposure is unevenly distributed across owners within organizational forms. Figure S3 plots Lorenz-style concentration curves for SFHA-located SFR parcels by organizational form. The curves indicate that SFHA holdings are comparatively diffuse among individual and trust owners, but much more concentrated among corporations and government/nonprofit owners, with LLC holdings also exhibiting substantial concentration.  

# 4.6 Residual spatial dependence and spatial Poisson robustness

Although neighborhood fixed effects absorb neighborhood-level mean differences and clustered standard errors address within-neighborhood residual correlation for inference, diagnostics indicate significant remaining spatial dependence in model residuals. Moran's I computed on the primary-model residuals using an 8-nearest-neighbor weights matrix is 0.392 (p < 0.001; Table 9).

To assess whether ownership–SFHA associations are robust to explicit spatial modeling, we implement eigenvector spatial filtering (ESF), a method that extracts eigenvectors from the spatial weights matrix and includes them as controls in the Poisson GLM to absorb spatially structured variation in the outcome (Griffith, 2003). In a sensitivity analysis varying the number of spatial filters from 15 to 200 (Table S7), residual Moran's I decreases from 0.39 (neighborhood FE only) to effectively zero (0.003) with 200 filters—a 99% reduction in residual spatial autocorrelation. Across all specifications, the LLC risk ratio remains stable and statistically significant: it attenuates modestly from 1.38 (FE only) to 1.24 (FE + 200 spatial filters), with p < 0.001 in all cases. This 10% attenuation indicates that approximately one-tenth of the unadjusted LLC–SFHA association reflects spatial confounding absorbed by the filters, while the remaining association is robust to spatial structure.

This pattern—stable coefficients across increasingly aggressive spatial controls—supports interpreting the ownership–SFHA associations as substantive conditional patterns rather than artifacts of unmodeled spatial dependence. Nevertheless, some residual clustering is expected given that SFHAs are defined by topography and hydrology; the estimated risk ratios are accordingly interpreted as conditional associations describing how mapped regulatory flood exposure is distributed across owner-of-record organizational forms and local portfolio footprints, rather than as causal effects.

# 4.7 Robustness checks

Key contrasts remain stable across alternative specifications. When owner–parcel distance is added as an additional covariate (M4), attenuation of the primary contrasts is minimal (1–2% reduction in log RR). Estimates are also robust to restricting the sample to high-confidence classifier labels (predicted probability ≥ 0.80) and to relaxing the SFHA exposure threshold from majority-area to 10% parcel overlap. Alternative standard error specifications—including owner-clustered and spatial HAC (Conley) standard errors—yield consistent inference for the main ownership contrasts. The eigenvector spatial filtering analysis (Section 4.6; Table S7) provides the strongest spatial robustness test: even when residual Moran's I is reduced to near zero, the LLC risk ratio remains elevated and significant (RR = 1.24, p < 0.001). Full robustness estimates are reported in Supplementary Tables S5 and S7.

# 4.8 Summary of findings

Across descriptive and adjusted analyses, SFHA parcels are not evenly distributed across ownership structures. Unadjusted rates show large differences in SFHA prevalence across organizational form × footprint groups (Table 6). In the preferred neighborhood fixed-effects specification with neighborhood-clustered inference (Table 7), LLC-held parcels—both single- and multi-parcel—remain reliably over-represented among SFHA parcels, and multi-parcel individuals and multi-parcel trusts also exhibit elevated SFHA prevalence relative to the reference group. Evidence for within-form footprint differences is weaker under clustered inference and multiple-testing adjustment (Table 8), but the overall pattern indicates systematic variation in who holds regulatory flood exposure across observable legal-form and within-county footprint dimensions.  

## 5. Discussion

This study demonstrates that regulatory flood exposure—measured as parcel location within FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs)—is not only a spatial attribute of the housing stock but also an organizational attribute of the ownership landscape. Although SFHA exposure is uncommon among single-family residential parcels in Douglas County, it is unevenly distributed across owner-of-record structures. In descriptive comparisons, SFHA prevalence varies sharply across organizational forms and local portfolio footprints, with especially high raw rates among single-parcel LLCs and among corporate and government/nonprofit parcels in both single- and multi-parcel categories (Table 7). In adjusted models that compare parcels within the same appraisal neighborhoods and control for assessed value and parcel size, LLC-owned parcels—both single-parcel and multi-parcel—retain elevated prevalence of SFHA location relative to the baseline of single-parcel individual owners (Table 8).

The governance relevance of these patterns is twofold. First, ownership structure provides information that a flood map alone cannot: it indicates the organizational units through which floodplain management, mitigation assistance, and post-event coordination must be implemented. Second, the concentration of SFHA holdings within organizational forms suggests that exposure is not only uneven across parcels but also uneven across owners, shaping whether policy engagement is necessarily diffuse (many small owners) or potentially tractable through targeted engagement with a limited number of portfolios (Figure S3).

# 5.1 Organizational form and local footprint as a governance layer

A central contribution of this analysis is to place a decision-relevant ownership typology—organizational form crossed with within-county portfolio footprint—into the same empirical frame as a regulatory hazard layer. In the descriptive data, individuals account for most single-family parcels in Douglas County, while LLCs constitute a substantial secondary owner category (Tables 2 and 5). These baseline distributions matter for interpretation: even when a form represents a minority of parcels, its holdings can still account for a disproportionate share of SFHA-located parcels if exposure is concentrated within that form or within particular footprint strata (Table 7; Figure S3).

In the preferred fixed-effects specification, the strongest and most stable adjusted association is the elevated SFHA prevalence among LLC-owned parcels. Both single-parcel and multi-parcel LLCs show higher adjusted prevalence than the reference group after accounting for parcel attributes and neighborhood context (Table 8). The models also reveal that ownership structure is not merely a business-entity phenomenon: multi-parcel individuals exhibit higher adjusted prevalence than single-parcel individuals, and multi-parcel trusts are also elevated relative to the baseline. Together, these results indicate that mapped regulatory exposure is organized across multiple dimensions of ownership, with both legal form and local footprint capturing meaningful differences in where exposure is held.

Within-form contrasts provide additional nuance but require careful interpretation. In the descriptive rates, single-parcel LLCs exhibit substantially higher SFHA prevalence than multi-parcel LLCs (Table 7), and the point estimates remain ordered in that direction in the adjusted models (Table 8). However, under neighborhood-clustered inference, the single–multi difference within LLCs is not statistically distinguishable at conventional thresholds (Table 9 in the revised analysis). The most defensible interpretation is therefore that LLC ownership—irrespective of whether the local footprint is one parcel or multiple parcels—is robustly associated with higher regulatory flood exposure in this setting, while evidence for distinct single-asset LLC behavior is suggestive rather than definitive in this cross-sectional snapshot.

# 5.2 Interpreting patterns without conflating association with mechanism

The results document conditional prevalence differences: they do not identify a causal effect of organizational form on floodplain siting. Floodplains are generated by hydrology and mapped through regulatory processes, and organizational form does not determine floodplain location. Instead, observed ownership patterns are consistent with processes of selection into, retention of, and organizational structuring around regulated hazard exposure. Several interpretations remain plausible given existing scholarship, but the present design cannot adjudicate among them.

One possibility is that imperfect risk capitalization and information frictions contribute to heterogeneous sorting into flood-exposed housing. Hedonic evidence suggests that flood exposure can be associated with price penalties in some contexts (Bin & Polasky, 2004; Atreya & Ferreira, 2015), while national evidence indicates that climate risk can remain insufficiently priced in aggregate (Gourevitch et al., 2023). These findings imply that market signals may be incomplete, and that some actors may be more willing or able to hold regulated exposure. However, this interpretation does not require the strong claim that flood-exposed parcels offer superior risk-adjusted returns on average. The more modest implication is that a mix of constraints and strategies—capital availability, diversification capacity, and operational specialization—can shape who holds SFHA-located parcels even when risk is not fully reflected in transaction prices or insurance signals.

A second possibility is that institutional capacity and portfolio operations help explain why certain owner structures appear more prevalent in SFHAs. Research on post-crisis investment in single-family housing documents systematic acquisition strategies and operational scaling among professionalized investors (Immergluck & Law, 2014a, 2014b; Colburn et al., 2021), and evidence on returns to single-family rentals underscores that yields and strategies vary across market segments (Demers & Eisfeldt, 2022). If SFHA-located parcels differ in price tier, redevelopment potential, maintenance demands, or compliance complexity, owners with greater administrative and operational capacity may be more likely to hold them. This channel is compatible with the elevated adjusted prevalence among LLCs and with the footprint gradients observed for individuals and trusts.

A third possibility involves legal structuring and administrative legibility. LLCs can function as legal shells that compartmentalize liability and complicate accountability in some housing contexts (Travis, 2019). The descriptive pattern of higher SFHA prevalence among single-parcel LLCs is consistent with the possibility that some high-exposure parcels are held in single-asset entities, but the revised within-form tests indicate that stronger claims about liability siloing require designs that observe acquisition timing, beneficial ownership linkages, or changes in entity form over time. The owner-of-record unit remains consequential for governance because it is the entity that appears in land records and can be the interface for enforcement, permitting, and program administration; nevertheless, beneficial ownership consolidation may be necessary for other questions, such as market power or coordinated portfolio strategy (An et al., 2024; Hangen & O’Brien, 2024; Shelton & Seymour, 2024; Torres, 2024).

Finally, the persistence of significant residual spatial dependence after neighborhood fixed effects and clustered inference underscores that unmeasured micro-spatial conditions remain correlated in ways that this design does not fully capture. This limitation reinforces an interpretation of the reported risk ratios as conditional descriptive associations that summarize how SFHA exposure is distributed across ownership structures within the county’s observed development and regulatory geography.

# 5.3 Implications for floodplain governance and urban analytics

The principal implication of these findings is not that floodplain standards, disclosure requirements, or insurance recommendations should vary by owner type. Rather, the implication is that the implementation of flood governance often depends on the organizational units that hold exposure and on how concentrated that exposure is across owners. From an administrative perspective, two places with identical SFHA parcel counts can pose very different governance challenges if one has exposure dispersed among many single-parcel households and the other has exposure concentrated in a smaller number of portfolios. The concentration patterns documented here highlight why the “who” dimension can be relevant to implementation capacity even when the regulatory rules apply uniformly.

The analysis also illustrates how routine administrative data can support ownership-aware hazard analytics without requiring a comprehensive beneficial ownership registry. Assessor files already encode the owner-of-record name, and these strings can be transformed into a tractable set of legal-form categories, enabling consistent descriptive comparisons across cities and over time. In this sense, the methodological contribution is best understood as an enabling infrastructure: it provides a reproducible way to add an ownership layer to regulatory hazard overlays, which can then be used to document whether exposure is diffuse or concentrated, whether certain owner structures systematically hold a greater share of mapped exposure, and how those patterns shift as markets and policies change.

More broadly, the results suggest that floodplain governance may benefit from integrating ownership structure into routine monitoring and program evaluation. For example, mitigation programs often rely on repeated contact, documentation, and follow-through. If SFHA holdings are concentrated among a limited number of owner-of-record entities within certain forms, administrative agencies and community partners may be able to design outreach strategies that are portfolio-aware while still applying uniform standards and maintaining equity safeguards. Conversely, where exposure is dispersed among single-parcel owners, the administrative burden shifts toward broader engagement and may require different delivery strategies. These are implementation questions rather than rule-differentiation questions, and they motivate ownership visibility as an input to governance planning rather than as a basis for unequal treatment.

# 5.4 Limitations and directions for future research

Several limitations follow directly from the data structure and the governance-forward measurement approach.

First, SFHA exposure is a regulatory boundary, not a comprehensive measure of flood hazard. SFHAs do not capture all flood modes and may not reflect future changes in climate risk. The study addresses boundary sensitivity with an alternative overlap rule, but extensions using additional hazard layers (e.g., pluvial susceptibility, depth grids, repeated-loss indicators) would strengthen inference about hazard exposure beyond regulatory designation. Additionally, the analysis is restricted to single-family residential parcels; commercial, industrial, and multifamily properties in SFHAs may exhibit different ownership patterns and are not examined here.

Second, the analysis relies on owner-of-record entities and within-county footprint. This choice is aligned with local administrative practice but does not observe holdings outside Douglas County or consolidate ownership across multiple legal shells. Future work can combine shell-level typologies with beneficial ownership linkage methods to assess how much the organization of exposure changes when entities are consolidated into networks or ultimate owners (An et al., 2024; Hangen & O’Brien, 2024; Shelton & Seymour, 2024; Torres, 2024). Such linkage would also help address how scale operates in regional housing markets that cross county boundaries.

Third, the design is cross-sectional. A single assessment roll cannot distinguish between acquisition, retention, and restructuring processes, nor can it evaluate how policy and insurance regime changes may alter behavior over time. In particular, FEMA's Risk Rating 2.0, implemented in 2021–2022, restructured flood insurance pricing to reflect property-specific risk factors; the 2022 snapshot analyzed here cannot distinguish ownership patterns that predate versus postdate this change (GAO, 2023; Ortega & Petkov 2024). Transaction-based designs linking deeds and sale dates to buyer legal form would allow tests of selection into SFHAs, and multi-year assessor panels could reveal trends in ownership composition and concentration of regulatory exposure. These designs would also make it possible to examine whether changes in flood insurance pricing or disclosure regimes coincide with changes in who holds mapped exposure, rather than inferring strategies from a single snapshot.

Finally, significant residual spatial dependence suggests that micro-spatial processes remain important even after neighborhood fixed effects. Future analyses could evaluate alternative spatial inference approaches and test sensitivity to different neighborhood definitions and spatial scales. Such work would complement the governance-oriented insight of the present study: ownership structure is a meaningful correlate of where regulatory exposure is held, but its interpretation must be situated within a strongly patterned spatial landscape.

Taken together, the results show that regulatory flood exposure is not simply a spatial attribute of the single-family housing stock; it is also organized through ownership structures that differ in legal form and local footprint. Making these structures legible in routine administrative data offers a practical way to connect floodplain analytics to the organizational units through which local governance is implemented. The conclusion reiterates the study’s core contribution and outlines how this ownership-aware approach can be extended in future work.

## 6. Conclusion

Regulatory flood maps are foundational tools for floodplain management, but they primarily describe where regulated hazard is located. This study adds a complementary perspective by making visible how that mapped exposure is organized across owners. Using the complete 2022 inventory of single-family residential parcels in Douglas County, Nebraska, we link FEMA Special Flood Hazard Areas (SFHAs) to an owner-of-record typology that distinguishes organizational form (individuals, LLCs, corporations, trusts, and government/nonprofit entities) and a within-county portfolio footprint based on the number of parcels held under the same legal name. SFHA exposure is uncommon in the county’s single-family stock (about 1.10% of parcels under the majority-area rule), yet it is not evenly distributed across ownership structures.  

Across both descriptive and adjusted models, LLC ownership is the most consistent correlate of higher regulatory flood exposure at the parcel level. In unadjusted comparisons, SFHA prevalence is substantially higher for single-parcel LLCs than for single-parcel individual owners (Table 7).  In the preferred within-neighborhood specification that controls for parcel value and parcel size and uses neighborhood-clustered inference, both single- and multi-parcel LLCs retain elevated SFHA prevalence relative to the reference category. Elevated prevalence is also observed for multi-parcel individuals and multi-parcel trusts, indicating that within-county footprint is informative beyond business entities. In contrast, evidence for sharply distinct single-asset behavior within LLCs is weaker under conservative clustered inference, highlighting the value of interpreting within-form contrasts as suggestive patterns in a cross-sectional snapshot rather than as direct tests of specific mechanisms.

Beyond average differences in SFHA prevalence, SFHA holdings are also unevenly distributed across owners within each organizational form. Concentration patterns (Lorenz/Gini) indicate that exposure is skewed within forms rather than uniformly spread across all owners, a fact that has direct relevance for implementation. Where exposure is concentrated, the administrative feasibility of program delivery and engagement may differ from contexts in which exposure is dispersed across many single-parcel owners, even when total exposure is similar.  

The primary contribution of this paper is therefore governance-oriented: it demonstrates that ownership structure is a meaningful layer for describing and monitoring regulatory flood exposure, and that this layer can be constructed from widely available assessor records in a reproducible way. The approach does not imply that floodplain standards should vary by owner type; rather, it shows that the organizational units through which uniform rules and programs are implemented are heterogeneous and measurable. A secondary contribution is methodological: by translating unstandardized owner-of-record strings into consistent legal-form categories and combining them with a local footprint measure, the workflow provides a portable basis for ownership-aware hazard analytics that can be replicated across jurisdictions and extended with additional data sources.

Several limitations point to clear next steps. SFHAs are a regulatory boundary and do not capture the full range of flood hazards, including pluvial flooding and future climate-driven changes. Owner-of-record entities are not the same as beneficial owners, and within-county footprint does not observe holdings outside the county or across multiple legal shells. Finally, the analysis is cross-sectional and residual spatial dependence remains detectable even after within-neighborhood comparisons, reinforcing the interpretation of results as conditional associations rather than causal effects. Future work should combine this ownership-layer approach with transaction histories, multi-year assessor panels, additional hazard layers, and beneficial-ownership linkage methods to evaluate how acquisition timing, insurance regimes, and consolidation strategies shape the organization of flood exposure over time.

Taken together, the results show that the "who" of ownership is empirically consequential for understanding the organization of mapped regulatory flood exposure. Integrating ownership structure into floodplain analytics provides a practical way to move from describing where exposure lies to characterizing how it is held—an essential step for linking hazard mapping to the realities of policy implementation.
